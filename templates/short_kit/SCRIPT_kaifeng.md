## Facts + sources
Primary: History of Jin (金史) ch. 113, biography of Chizhan Hexi (赤盞合喜), https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/金史/卷113
- "每城一角置砲百餘枝，更遞下上，晝夜不息，不數日，石幾與裏城平。" Over a hundred trebuchets at each corner of the
  city, taking turns day and night; within days the stones were piled almost level with the inner wall.
- "大兵用砲則不然，破大磑或碌碡爲二三，皆用之。" The Mongols broke big millstones and stone rollers into two or
  three pieces and fired them all.
- "父老所傳周世宗築京城，取虎牢土爲之，堅密如鐵，受砲所擊唯凹而已。" Elders said Emperor Shizong of (Later) Zhou
  built the wall with earth from Hulao; it was dense as iron, and trebuchet hits only dented it.
- "城上樓櫓皆故宮及芳華、玉溪所拆大木爲之，合抱之木，隨擊而碎，以馬糞麥秸布其上，綱索旃褥固護之。" The towers on
  the wall were built from timber taken from old palaces; trunks as thick as a two-arm hug shattered when hit, so they
  covered them with horse dung and wheat straw, held with rope nets and felt mattresses.
- "龍德宮造砲石，取宋太湖、靈璧假山爲之。" The defenders made their own trebuchet stones from the old Song emperor's
  Taihu and Lingbi garden rockery.
- "以鐵繩懸「震天雷」者，順城而下，至掘處火發，人與牛皮皆碎迸無跡。" Thunder-crash bombs lowered on iron ropes down
  the wall to where the Mongols dug under cowhide; the cowhide was blown away (shown non-graphic).
- "至是十六晝夜矣……大兵知不可下，乃謾爲好語云。" After 16 days and nights the Mongols knew they could not take it
  and offered (insincere) peace talks.
Secondary: Wikipedia "Mongol siege of Kaifeng" (citing Franke and Allsen, Cambridge History of China vol. 6; Turnbull
2003; Needham vol. 5 pt 7), Britannica "Mongol siege of Kaifeng".
- Siege from 8 April 1232. Subutai commanded the combined Mongol army (Ögedei and Tolui fell ill and left).
- Sappers dug toward the walls under cowhide shields; Jin bombs and fire lances.
- During the talks, plague and starvation spread inside; supplies ran out.
- Emperor Aizong left late 1232. General Cui Li, left in charge, opened the gates; the Mongols entered 29 May 1233.
- Subutai had beaten the Rus' and Cumans at the Kalka River (1223) and fought across north China: "from China to Russia".
Physics: stone and fired brick are stiff and brittle, so a big hit drives a crack through the block; compacted earth is
a mass of small grains, so a hit crushes the grains it touches and the energy is spent in a local dent. Gunpowder-era
sources make the same point for earth-backed walls (warhistory.org "The Influence of Gunpowder Weapons on
Fortification"). Ming walls kept rammed-earth cores behind brick faces (Jiayuguan, Xi'an, Nanjing).

## Script v1 (~241 words, ~65 s; "You see" at word ~171 = 70 %; resolve >= 45 s; set piece 10-13 s)
In 1232, Genghis Khan's greatest general, Subutai, threw boulders at one Chinese city wall for sixteen days and nights.
The wall was made of dirt. And it never broke.
Subutai had crushed armies from China to Russia.
So at every corner of the city, he set up a hundred catapults.            <- SET PIECE 10-13 s (crane-up: 400 trebuchets fire)
They fired day and night.
When they ran out of rocks, they smashed millstones and fired those.
The rubble piled up almost as high as the wall.
The wall? Just dents.
The wooden towers on top weren't so lucky, so the defenders padded them with horse dung, straw and felt blankets.
Then they fired back an old emperor's fancy garden rocks.
Mongol diggers crept up under cowhide tents, so the defenders lowered iron bombs on chains.
Boom. No more tent.
Luckily, after sixteen days, the Mongols gave up and offered peace. Unluckily, the city had already run out of food.
So how did a wall of dirt beat the best siege army on Earth?
You see, stone is hard but brittle, like a plate. One big hit and it cracks right through.
Pounded dirt is billions of tiny grains. A boulder only crushes the grains it touches, and the blow dies in a dent, like a fist in a pillow.
Even under their bricks, China's walls kept a heart of pounded dirt.
And the Mongols? A year later they walked right in. A general inside simply opened the gate.   <- loops to frame 0
